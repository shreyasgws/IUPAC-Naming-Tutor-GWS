What is Organic Chemistry?

Organic chemistry is the study of compounds made primarily from carbon atoms. Carbon is special because it forms exactly 4 covalent bonds, allowing it to link with other carbons to build chains, branches, and rings. This produces millions of different compounds — more than any other element can produce. Because there are so many, a universal naming system is essential so that every chemist in every country can identify the same compound by its name alone.

Examples:

  • CH4 → methane (1 carbon, simplest organic compound)
  • C2H6 → ethane (2 carbons, carbon linked to carbon)
  • C6H6 → benzene (6 carbons in a ring)

Carbon forms 4 bonds. This tetravalency is the single most important fact in all of organic chemistry.

The IUPAC System — What It Is and Why It Matters

IUPAC = International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry. The IUPAC system guarantees: one compound = one unique name, and one name = one unique structure. No ambiguity. Before IUPAC, compounds had random common names (acetone, vinegar, wood alcohol) that meant nothing structurally. IUPAC fixed this.

The Three-Part Name Structure — memorise this formula before anything else:

[ PREFIX ] + [ ROOT ] + [ SUFFIX ]

PREFIX = substituents (branches, extra groups)

ROOT = the length of the main carbon chain

SUFFIX = the functional group or bond type

Concrete Preview Example: 2-methylpropan-1-ol

  • → prefix: 2-methyl (a methyl branch at carbon 2)
  • → root: prop (3-carbon chain)
  • → suffix: -an-1-ol (single bonds, alcohol at carbon 1)

Learn this three-part formula now. Every single IUPAC name follows it.

The Four Ways to Write an Organic Compound

The same compound can be represented four different ways. You must be able to move between them fluently.

TypeWhat It ShowsExample (Butane)
MolecularAtom count onlyC4H10

Full StructuralEvery bond drawnH–C–C–C–C–H with all H
Bond-Line/SkeletalLines only, H atoms hidden

Bond-line (skeletal) is the most common representation in professional chemistry. Master it first.

Bond-Line (Skeletal) Structure — The 5 Rules

Bond-line is the most common way chemists draw structures. You must be able to read it instantly.

Rule 1: Each corner and each endpoint = one carbon atom.

No carbon symbol is written. It is implied.

A zigzag with 4 vertices = 4 carbons = butane

Rule 2: Hydrogen atoms attached to carbon are NOT shown.

Each carbon has 4 bonds total. The hydrogens fill whatever is left over.

  • A line endpoint with 1 bond shown → has 3 hidden H atoms (CH3)
  • A corner with 2 bonds shown → has 2 hidden H atoms (CH2)
  • A corner with 3 bonds shown → has 1 hidden H atom (CH)
  • A carbon with 4 bonds shown → has 0 hidden H atoms (C)

Rule 3: Non-carbon and non-hydrogen atoms MUST be written explicitly.

This includes: O, N, Cl, Br, I, F, S.

AtomSymbolExample GroupCanvas
OxygenO–OH (alcohol)
NitrogenN–NH2 (amine)
ChlorineCl–Cl (chloro)
BromineBr–Br (bromo)
SulfurS–SH (thiol)

Rule 4: Multiple bonds are shown as multiple parallel lines.

Bond TypeDrawingExampleCanvas
Single (—)one lineEthane
Double (=)two linesEthene
Triple (≡)three linesEthyne

Rule 5: Functional groups with multiple atoms are written at their position.

  • Aldehyde (–CHO): written as CHO at the chain end.
  • Ketone (>C=O): double bond to O shown on the chain.
  • Carboxylic acid (–COOH): written as COOH at the end.
  • Alcohol (–OH): written as OH at the relevant position.

Rule 3 is the most commonly forgotten. If you don't write O, N, Cl, etc. explicitly, the structure is unreadable.

Bond Angles in Bond-Line Structure

The zigzag shape in bond-line drawings is not random. It reflects the real bond angles of each carbon type.

  • 109.5° (tetrahedral) — single bonds only. Carbon is sp3 hybridised.
  • 120° (trigonal planar) — carbon has a double bond. sp2 hybridised.
  • 180° (linear) — carbon has a triple bond. sp hybridised.

If you know the bond angle, you know the hybridisation. 109.5° = sp³, 120° = sp², 180° = sp.

How to Count Carbons in Bond-Line Structures

Method 1: Count every corner and every endpoint.

  • Line with 2 endpoints only = 2 carbons (ethane)
  • Zigzag with 1 corner + 2 endpoints = 3 carbons (propane)
  • Zigzag with 3 corners + 2 endpoints = 5 carbons (pentane)

Method 2: Count the number of line segments.

5 line segments = 5 carbons = pentane

Method 3: Multiple bonds do NOT add new carbons.

Double bond between 2 points = 2 carbons = ethene

Branches in Bond-Line Structure

Branches are lines coming off the main chain at angles. Each branch endpoint is also a carbon.

2-Methylbutane: 4-carbon chain, methyl branch at C2.

2,2-Dimethylpropane (Neopentane): central carbon with 4 methyl branches.

3-Ethyl-2-methylpentane: 5-carbon chain, ethyl at C3, methyl at C2.

Bond Types and Their Suffixes

The type of bond between carbons determines the suffix of the name.

BondSymbolTypeSuffixExample Name

DoubleC=CAlkene-eneEthene

  • CH2=CH2 → 2 carbons, double bond → eth + ene → Ethene
  • CH≡CH → 2 carbons, triple bond → eth + yne → Ethyne

These three suffixes (-ane, -ene, -yne) are the most fundamental in IUPAC naming. Everything else builds on them.

Functional Groups — What They Are

A functional group is a specific atom or group of atoms attached to the carbon chain that gives the molecule its chemical properties. The functional group determines the suffix (or prefix) of the IUPAC name.

Think of it like this: the carbon chain is the body, and the functional group is the personality.

The Prefix and Suffix System — The Core Idea

In IUPAC naming, every functional group has two possible roles:

ROLE 1 — SUFFIX (Main group): Used for the highest priority functional group only. Written at the END of the name.
Example: –OH as main group → ethanol (the "-ol" is the suffix)

ROLE 2 — PREFIX (Supporting group): Used for all other functional groups that are present but not the highest priority.
Example: –OH as supporting group → hydroxy- (e.g. 2-hydroxypropanal)

SUFFIX = The King. Only one compound can wear the crown. The highest priority group gets the suffix.

PREFIX = The Helpers. All other groups become prefixes.

The Complete Naming Algorithm — Overview First

Before explaining each step individually, here is the complete algorithm. See the big picture first:

STEP 1 — Identify all functional groups present.

STEP 2 — Select the highest priority functional group.

STEP 3 — Find the longest continuous carbon chain that includes the main group.

STEP 4 — Count carbons → assign root word.

STEP 5 — Number the chain (lowest locant to most important feature).

STEP 6 — Identify all substituents and their positions.

STEP 7 — Arrange substituents alphabetically.

STEP 8 — Apply multiplying prefixes (di-, tri-, tetra-).

STEP 9 — Assemble full name with correct punctuation.

Each section below explains one step in detail with worked examples.

Step 1 — Identify Functional Groups

Before naming anything, scan the entire structure and list every functional group. Use the reference table from Unit 2.

Structure: HO–CH2–CH2–CHO

Groups: –OH (alcohol) and –CHO (aldehyde)

Structure: CH3–CH(Cl)–CH2–OH

Groups: –Cl (chloro) and –OH (alcohol)

If you miss a functional group at Step 1, your final name WILL be wrong.

Step 2 — Select the Highest Priority Group

When multiple functional groups are present, only one becomes the suffix. The rest become prefixes.

1 –COOH (Carboxylic acid) ← HIGHEST

2 –CHO (Aldehyde)

3 >C=O (Ketone)

4 –OH (Alcohol)

5 –NH2 (Amine)

6 C=C (Alkene)

7 C≡C (Alkyne)

8 –X, –NO2, –R (Always prefixes)

–OH and –COOH: COOH beats OH. Suffix: -oic acid. OH → hydroxy-.

–CHO and >C=O: CHO beats C=O. Suffix: -al. Ketone → oxo-.

Step 3 — Select the Longest Carbon Chain (Parent Chain)

The parent chain is the longest CONTINUOUS chain that: (a) includes the main group, (b) includes any C=C or C≡C, (c) if equal, choose the one with MORE substituents.

CH3–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH3: Longest chain = 4C = but-

CH3–CH2–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH3: Longest chain = 5C = pent-

Tie-breaker — equal length chains: two 5C chains exist; pick the one with more substituents.

Chain A (2 substituents)

Chain B (1 substituent)

→ Chain A wins (more substituents).

MOST COMMON MISTAKE: Choosing the visually straight line instead of tracing ALL possible carbon paths.

Step 4 — Assign the Root Word

Count the carbons in the parent chain and look up the root word.

  • 3 carbons → root = prop
  • 6 carbons → root = hex
  • 10 carbons → root = dec

Step 5 — Number the Chain (The Locant Rule)

A locant is a position number. Number from the end that gives the LOWEST number to the most important feature.

Priority Order for Numbering

1st: Lowest number to highest priority functional group.

2nd: Lowest number to double/triple bond.

3rd: Lowest number to the first substituent.

Functional group wins: CH3–CH2–CH2–OH

Number from right → OH at C1 → propan-1-ol ✓

Double bond: CH2=CH–CH2–CH3

From left → but-1-ene ✓

Substituent: CH3–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH3

From left → methyl at C2 → 2-methylbutane ✓

The Lowest Set Rule

Compare sets left to right. At the first difference, the smaller number wins.

Lowest Set Rule example: 2,4-dimethyl vs 2,3,7-trimethyl

Option A: 2,4,5 vs Option B: 2,3,7

Position 1: 2 vs 2 → tied. Position 2: 4 vs 3 → 3 wins. Option B correct.

First Point of Difference: At the first position where locants differ, the lower number wins.

Compare (1,2,3) vs (1,2,4) → position 3 beats position 4.

Do NOT add up locants! Compare left to right, one number at a time. 2,4,5 sum=11 vs 2,3,7 sum=12 — pick 2,3,7 (position-by-position 4 vs 3 loses).

Step 6 — Identify and Name Substituents

Substituents are atoms or groups branching off the parent chain. Each is named with its standard group name and locant.

GroupSubstituent Name
–CH3methyl
–C2H5ethyl
–C3H7propyl
–Clchloro
–Brbromo
–Iiodo
–Ffluoro
–NO2nitro
–NH2amino (prefix)
–OHhydroxy (prefix)

CH3–CH(CH3)–CH3: methyl at C2 → 2-methylpropane

CH3–CH(Cl)–CH3: Cl at C2 → 2-chloropropane

Step 7 — Alphabetical Order of Substituents

When multiple substituents exist, list them alphabetically.

DO count: methyl, ethyl, propyl, chloro, bromo, iso-, neo-, cyclo-.

DO NOT count (ignore): di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, sec-, tert-.

Ethyl and methyl: E before M → 3-ethyl-2-methylpentane

With di- ignored: ethyl and dimethyl → compare E vs M → ethyl first

Quick test: "dimethyl" vs "ethyl" — ETHYL first. Ignore "di", compare M vs E.

Step 8 — Multiple Identical Substituents (di-, tri-, tetra-)

If the same substituent appears more than once, use a multiplying prefix.

  • 2 → di-
  • 3 → tri-
  • 4 → tetra-

Two methyls at C2 and C3: 2,3-dimethylbutane

Three methyls at C2,2,4: 2,2,4-trimethylpentane

Step 9 — Write the Full Name (Formatting Rules)

  • Commas between numbers: 2,3-dimethyl
  • Hyphens between numbers and words: 2-methylbutane
  • No spaces anywhere
  • Drop final 'e' before vowel suffix: propan-1-ol

Full assembly: methyl at C2, ethyl at C3, 5C chain (pent), single bonds (ane)

Alphabetical: ethyl before methyl

Final name: 3-ethyl-2-methylpentane

⚡ Unit 3 Complete — Core Algorithm Mastered

  1. Find functional groups
  2. Highest priority group
  3. Longest chain (includes main group)
  4. Root word
  5. Number chain (lowest locants)
  6. Name substituents
  7. Alphabetise (ignore di/tri, include iso/neo)
  8. di/tri/tetra for repeated groups
  9. Assemble with commas and hyphens

Why Priority Order Matters

When a molecule contains two or more different functional groups, three decisions must be made: (a) Which group determines the suffix? (b) Which groups become prefixes? (c) Which end do you number from? All three are answered by one rule: the priority ladder.

Same atoms, different priority outcome:

COOH wins → 3-hydroxypropanoic acid

Ketone beats OH → 1-hydroxypropan-2-one

The Priority Ladder — Full Reference

HIGHEST (always suffix if present)

1 –COOH Carboxylic acid → -oic acid

2 –CHO Aldehyde → -al

3 >C=O Ketone → -one

4 –OH Alcohol → -ol

5 –NH2 Amine → -amine

6 C=C Alkene → -ene

7 C≡C Alkyne → -yne

8 –X, –NO2, –R → ALWAYS prefix

LOWEST (always prefix, never suffix)

Example molecules at each priority level:

Rank 1 (acid): ethanoic acid

Rank 2 (aldehyde): propanal

Rank 3 (ketone): propanone

Rank 4 (alcohol): propan-1-ol

Applying the Priority Ladder — 7 Worked Scenarios

Scenario 1: Carboxylic Acid + Alcohol HO–CH2–CH2–COOH

COOH (rank 1) → -oic acid. OH → hydroxy-.

Name: 3-hydroxypropanoic acid

Scenario 2: Aldehyde + Ketone CH3–CO–CH2–CHO

CHO (rank 2) → -al. Ketone → oxo-.

Name: 3-oxobutanal

Scenario 3: Alcohol + Ketone CH3–CO–CH2–OH

Ketone (rank 3) beats OH (rank 4).

Name: 1-hydroxypropan-2-one

Scenario 4: Only Multiple Bonds CH2=CH–CH=CH2

Suffix: -diene. Name: buta-1,3-diene

Scenario 5: Halogen + Alcohol CH3–CH(Cl)–CH2–OH

OH (rank 4) → -ol. Cl → chloro-.

Name: 2-chloropropan-1-ol

Scenario 6: Amine + Alcohol HO–CH2–CH2–NH2

OH (rank 4) beats NH2 (rank 5).

Name: 2-aminoethan-1-ol

Scenario 7: Carboxylic Acid + Everything

CH3–CH(OH)–COOH → COOH dominates.

Name: 2-hydroxypropanoic acid

Functional Group in the Parent Chain (Important Trap)

The parent chain MUST include the highest priority functional group.

CH3–CH(CH3)–CH2–CHO

Chain including CHO = 4C (but-). CHO must be in chain.

Name: 3-methylbutanal

Same Functional Group Repeated

If the same functional group appears more than once, use multiplying prefixes for the suffix too.

HO–CH2–CH2–OH (two alcohols)

Name: ethane-1,2-diol

HOOC–CH2–COOH (two carboxylic acids)

Name: propanedioic acid

  • All other groups become prefixes

Naming Alkenes and Alkynes

For alkenes (-ene) and alkynes (-yne), the position of the multiple bond must be specified using a locant. Format: [root]-[position]-[suffix].

CH2=CH–CH2–CH3 → but-1-ene

CH3–CH=CH–CH3 → but-2-ene

CH≡C–CH2–CH3 → but-1-yne

When Both Double AND Triple Bond Are Present

Include both suffixes. Write -en- before -yne. Format: [root]-[ene pos]-en-[yne pos]-yne

CH2=CH–C≡CH

Double bond at C1, triple bond at C3

Name: but-1-en-3-yne

Always: ene position BEFORE yne in the name.

Multiple Double Bonds (Dienes, Trienes, Diynes)

Two double bonds → -diene. Three double bonds → -triene. Two triple bonds → -diyne.

CH2=CH–CH=CH2 → double bonds at C1 and C3

Name: buta-1,3-diene

CH≡C–C≡CH → triple bonds at C1 and C3

Name: buta-1,3-diyne

Branched Substituents (Complex Substituents)

Sometimes a substituent attached to the main chain is itself branched.

  1. Identify the substituent's own carbon chain.
  2. Number from the carbon attached to the main chain (that carbon = position 1).
  3. Write the substituent's full name in brackets.
  4. Place the bracket-name with its locant in the main name.

Main chain: 6C (hexane). At C3: –CH(CH3)–CH3

Substituent name: (1-methylethyl)

Name: 3-(1-methylethyl)hexane

Substituent: –CH2–CH(CH3)–CH3

Name: (2-methylpropyl)

Always check: is your complex substituent actually part of a longer parent chain?

iso-, neo-, sec-, tert- Prefixes

Branching at the second-to-last carbon.

Two methyl groups on the second-to-last carbon.

Polyfunctional Compounds — Advanced Priority Applications

Full worked problem: HO–CH2–CH(CH3)–CHO

CHO > OH → suffix: -al. Chain: 3C (prop-).

Number from CHO: CHO=C1, CH(CH3)=C2, CH2OH=C3.

Substituents: methyl at C2, hydroxy at C3.

Alphabetical: hydroxy before methyl.

Name: 3-hydroxy-2-methylpropanal

Acid + Ketone + Alcohol: HOOC–CH2–CH(OH)–CO–CH3

COOH (rank 1) beats ketone (rank 3) and OH (rank 4).

Name: 3-hydroxy-4-oxopentanoic acid

Acid + Double bond: CH2=CH–CH2–COOH

COOH wins. Chain: 4C.

Name: but-3-enoic acid

Vowel Dropping Rule

Drop final -e of root before a vowel suffix.

The Complete Naming Algorithm — Final Checklist

STEP 1: Identify every functional group.

STEP 2: Rank by priority. Highest = suffix. COOH > CHO > CO > OH > NH2 > C=C > C≡C > X

STEP 3: Longest chain including main group and any double/triple bond.

STEP 4: Count carbons → root word (meth/eth/prop…dec).

STEP 5: Number chain. Lowest locant to: main group → bond → substituent. Position-by-position comparison.

STEP 6: Identify all substituents and their positions.

STEP 7: Alphabetical order. Ignore: di-, tri-, tetra-, sec-, tert-. Include: iso-, neo-, cyclo-.

STEP 8: di-/tri-/tetra- for repeated substituents.

STEP 9: Write name: commas between numbers, hyphens number-to-word. No spaces. Drop -e before vowel suffix.

The 10 Most Common Exam Mistakes

Mistake 1: Choosing the visually straight line as parent chain. Trace ALL carbon paths.

Mistake 2: Using SUM of locants to compare numbering. Compare left to right, one at a time.

Mistake 3: Forgetting to include functional group in parent chain. The parent MUST contain the main group.

Mistake 4: Numbering from the wrong end. Always number from the end that gives the main group the lowest number.

Mistake 5: Including di-/tri- when alphabetising. Ignore them — compare the base name.

Mistake 6: Wrong formatting — spaces or missing punctuation. 2,3-dimethylbutane (not 2 3 dimethyl butane).

Mistake 7: Confusing phenyl and benzyl. Phenyl = direct ring. Benzyl = ring + CH2 spacer.

Mistake 8: Forgetting halogens are always prefixes. –Cl, –Br, –I, –F are NEVER suffixes.

Mistake 9: Double bond position not stated. Always include locant for 4+ carbon chains.

Mistake 10: Choosing shorter chain to avoid hard substituent. The longest chain is ALWAYS the parent.
